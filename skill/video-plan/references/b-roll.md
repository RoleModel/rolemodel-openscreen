# B-Roll

B-roll is where a video gets texture, and it is the one visual element offered as **recommendation, not requirement**. Screen capture is locked to the script. B-roll is creative, and whoever edits gets to decide.

That distinction changes how you write it. A b-roll entry is a concept with a reason and a suggested placement, plus an alternative in case the footage does not exist. It is not an instruction.

## How To Write A Recommendation

```
Beat [n], around [m:ss]
  Concept:   [what the shot shows]
  Why here:  [what it does for this specific moment in the script]
  Feel:      [what it should communicate]
  Source:    [existing footage / needs capture / stock / motion graphic]
  Priority:  [strong recommendation / nice to have]
  Instead:   [an alternative if this is not available]
```

**Always include the alternative.** A recommendation that requires a shoot nobody has scheduled is not usable. The alternative is usually a motion graphic, existing footage, or holding on the speaker.

**Always say why here.** "Shop floor footage" is a stock request. "Shop floor footage while the script says the estimate is stale before it ships, so the viewer sees the work that the stale number is describing" is a creative recommendation an editor can act on or improve.

## What B-Roll Is Doing

B-roll earns its place when it does one of these. When it does none of them, it is decoration and it costs attention.

**Showing what the words describe.** The script names a shop floor, a job site, a warehouse. Show it. This is the most common and most reliable use.

**Giving the viewer's eyes somewhere to go.** A talking head holding longer than about 20 seconds starts to feel static. A cutaway resets attention without changing what the viewer is hearing.

**Covering an edit.** Two takes joined together jump. B-roll over the seam hides it. This is the practical reason editors want more b-roll than the plan calls for, and it is worth planning one or two safety cutaways per section.

**Compressing time.** "Six weeks later" is a caption over a cutaway. The alternative is words the script cannot afford.

**Carrying emotion the script will not say out loud.** People at work, hands on tools, a team in a room. RoleModel voice does not claim warmth, so the footage can.

## Concept Sources, Cheapest First

Ordered by how likely it is to actually happen.

**1. Existing footage.** Ask what has already been shot before recommending anything new. Prior videos, conference recordings, partner site visits, photography. The best b-roll recommendation is one that is already on a drive.

**2. Motion graphics.** A number animating, a diagram assembling, a process moving left to right. Costs no shoot day, stays on brand, and `/hyperframes-helper` can build it. This is the default alternative when live footage is not available.

**3. Screen capture as texture.** Not a demo. UI moving in the background, out of focus, or in an inset while the speaker talks. Already available if the demo captures are being recorded anyway.

**4. Simple new capture.** Hands on a keyboard or tablet, a whiteboard being drawn, a phone being checked, a laptop closing. Fifteen minutes with a phone camera. Realistic to ask for.

**5. A real location shoot.** A partner's shop floor, a job site, a manufacturing line. Highest value and highest cost. Recommend it only when the plan can support it, and always give an alternative.

**6. Stock.** Last resort. Generic stock footage reads as generic and undercuts a voice built on specificity. If stock is the only option, pick something concrete and textural rather than people-in-a-meeting.

## Placement

**Do not open on b-roll.** The open defines the problem or the purpose, and it lands hardest from a person or from clear on-screen text. B-roll in the first three seconds of a sound-off autoplay video is three seconds of nothing.

**Do not cut away during the demo.** The demo is the point. Leaving it breaks the thing the beat exists for.

**Do not cut away on the action.** The close is the one moment the viewer must be looking at the ask. Hold on the speaker or the end card.

**Cut away inside a belief, not between beliefs.** Section transitions are already a visual change. Adding b-roll there doubles up. The useful cutaway is mid-belief, while a claim is being supported.

**Roughly one cutaway per 20 to 30 seconds of talking head.** A guide, not a rule. Under 15 seconds and the video feels nervous. Over 40 and it feels static.

**Every cutaway is 2 to 5 seconds.** Longer than 5 and the viewer wonders whether the speaker is coming back.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Stock footage of people in a meeting pointing at a laptop.** Reads as filler in every video it has ever appeared in.
- **B-roll that contradicts the script.** Footage of a clean modern shop while the script describes a chaotic manual process.
- **Cutting away from a number.** When the script says a figure, the figure should be on screen, not a warehouse.
- **A cutaway with no relationship to the words.** The viewer spends the shot trying to work out the connection instead of listening.
- **Drone footage as an opener.** It is beautiful and it says nothing.
- **B-roll used to pad runtime.** If the video needs padding, the video is done and should be shorter.
